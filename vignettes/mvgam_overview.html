mvgam fits Bayesian dynamic generalized additive models and helps with plotting and forecasting them. The observation model is written as a brms formula and compiled through the brms Stan code generator; mvgam extends that pipeline with a latent state layer so the fitted model is a state-space model rather than a regression. An observation process maps the latent state to the response through a chosen likelihood, and a trend process governs how the latent state evolves over time.

Because the observation side is compiled by brms, the full brms modelling vocabulary carries through into state-space fits. Linear terms, smooths (s(), t2(), te()), Gaussian process smooths (gp()), monotonic effects (mo()), random effects through (... | ...), smooths split by a factor (by =), and nonlinear formulas through bf(..., nl = TRUE) all work on either side of the model. Joint responses compose through bf() arms with distributional regression on auxiliary parameters (e.g. bf(y ~ ..., sigma ~ ...)).

mvgam then adds the latent state layer. Inside trend_formula sit random walks, autoregressions with arbitrary sparse lag sets, vector autoregressions, piecewise growth and hierarchical residual correlations, alongside a continuous time AR for irregular sampling and a low-rank factor structure for joint species distribution models. Smooths and covariate effects can run on the latent state alongside the response, so the same modelling vocabulary applies in both layers.

Once fitted, the resulting object plugs into the wider Bayesian tooling: marginaleffects::predictions(), comparisons(), hypotheses() and slopes() dispatch on mvgam objects through the standard posterior_epred(), posterior_predict() and posterior_linpred() methods. bayesplot and loo plug in directly. as_draws() from the posterior package returns the draws as an array. tidy() and glance() return broom-format data frames, with augment() for adding posterior summaries back to the original data.

This vignette walks through the package on a simulated dataset generated by sim_mvgam(). The simulator returns the true smooth functions and latent trajectory it drew from, so every component’s recovery can be checked directly. The pedagogical question is sharp: when the data carries both a deterministic seasonal cycle and a stochastic dynamic process beyond that cycle, what does a fixed-seasonality smooth miss, and what does adding a latent state buy?

State-space formulation

For an observation \(y_{i, t}\) on series \(i\) at time \(t\) with link function \(g\) and observation family \(\mathcal{F}\):

\[\begin{align*} y_{i, t} & \sim \mathcal{F}(\tilde{y}_{i, t}, \phi) \\ g(\tilde{y}_{i, t}) & = \alpha_i + \sum_{j=1}^{J} s_{i, j}(x_{j, t}) + Z_{i, \cdot} \, z_{\cdot, t} \, . \end{align*}\]

The intercept \(\alpha_i\) and smooths \(s_{i, j}\) form the observation-side linear predictor. The latent state \(z_{k, t}\) evolves through a trend process (AR(1), VAR, RW, CAR and so on); \(Z\) is a loadings matrix mapping the \(K\)-dimensional latent state onto the \(N\) series. With \(K = N\) and \(Z = I\) each series gets its own latent state; with \(K < N\) the latent states are shared factors. Smooths and covariate effects passed through trend_formula sit on the latent state; everything in the top-level formula sits on the response.

Modelling APIs

Two fitters at the top of the package expose the same state-space machinery. They differ only in how the data are laid out and what the response represents.

mvgam() takes a long-format data frame indexed by a time column and (optionally) by a series column. The defaults are time and series, but every trend constructor accepts time = / series = arguments that point at any column in the data, so existing column names need not be renamed. The series dimension drives the multivariate trend constructors: VAR(), the cor = TRUE switches and hierarchical correlations through gr. The response is a single column on the brms side. Use this fitter to forecast one or more time series, including the closure-unit families when each unit produces a scalar response.

jsdgam() is the entry point for joint species distribution and closure-unit fits where the grouping is biological rather than temporal. Data arrive as one row per (unit, species) cell. Joint multivariate response families work on sites by species; occupancy and N-mixture models work on sites by visit by species, with the detection process attached as a second bf() arm. jsdgam() composes the same trend pipeline as mvgam(), with factor structure via n_lv and loadings_prior, and adds phylogenetic distance matrices through phylo and trait by environment interactions through trait_formula.

The family table below applies to both fitters. The closure-unit and joint multivariate response families work in either, and the choice between mvgam() and jsdgam() is driven by what the rows of the data represent rather than by the likelihood itself.

The complete brms family list is available through the integration layer. mvgam adds a small library of families for time-series and ecological use cases that brms does not ship natively:

Each family is documented on its own help page with a worked recovery example. The two groups that need extra explanation are the closure-unit families and the joint multivariate response families, both summarised below.

Closure-unit families: occ() and nmix()

Closure-unit data hold repeated observations of the same sampling unit (a site, a visit, a season) under a process that may detect the underlying state imperfectly. occ() and nmix() marginalise the latent occupancy or abundance state inside the Stan likelihood, so the chain samples only the detection model and the linear predictor for the state. Both enter through a second bf() arm.

occ() implements the single-season occupancy model of MacKenzie et al. (2002), which separates occupancy probability \(\psi\) from per-visit detection probability. nmix() supports three observation processes chosen at the constructor: "poisson_binomial" follows Royle (2004), "royle_nichols" follows Royle and Nichols (2003), and "poisson_poisson" treats each observed count as a noisy Poisson realisation of an underlying Poisson abundance.

Joint multivariate response families

These families model a multivariate response on the closure-unit grain. The likelihood is evaluated at the unit level (one row per (unit, species) cell with K species), and the trend pipeline composes through the latent state as for any other family. Two complementary identification mechanisms are needed because both compositional and continuous multivariate likelihoods are non-identified without them.

diri(), multi() and categ() model compositional or categorical responses on the simplex. The likelihood subtracts the reference category’s linear predictor from every entry of the length-\(K\) mu vector (a softmax shift equivalent to the \(K - 1\) reference parameterisation), and a sum_to_zero_vector[K] constraint is applied to each column of \(Z\). Together these identify the joint model without user-supplied constraints. posterior_epred() returns one probability per (unit, species) row summing to 1 within each unit.

mvn() and mvt() model continuous multivariate responses. The residual covariance is the sum of a loadings outer product and a diagonal of species-specific variances \(\Psi^2\):

\[\begin{align*} \Sigma & = Z Z^\top + \mathrm{diag}(\Psi^2) \end{align*}\]

residual_cor() converts this \(\Sigma\) to a correlation matrix between species on the response scale. The loadings are identified by the same rotation described in the trend factor model section below.

See ?jsdgam for the full surface, including the phylogenetic and trait-environment hooks, and ?ordinate.mvgam / ?ordinate.jsdgam for biplot helpers built on the rotated loadings.

Latent trend processes

Six families of trend constructor live inside trend_formula. Each is documented on its own help page; the formulas below give the per-step transition that the constructor compiles into Stan. The presentation starts with the simplest case (random walks), then covers stationary autoregressions, an analogue for irregular sampling, and a deterministic piecewise kernel.

Random walks: RW()

The simplest dynamic process. Each series carries a non-stationary state that drifts by a Normal innovation at every step. Setting ma = TRUE adds a moving-average error term \(\theta_i\) that lets the chain absorb brief shocks without forcing them into the next state:

\[\begin{align*} z_{i, t} & = z_{i, t - 1} + \theta_i \, \epsilon_{i, t - 1} + \epsilon_{i, t} \\ \epsilon_{i, t} & \sim \text{Normal}(0, \sigma_i) \end{align*}\]

Autoregressions: AR(p = ...)

AR() scales the previous state by lag coefficients \(\rho_l\). When p is a scalar, the lag set is \(\mathcal{L} = \{1, \dots, p\}\); when p is a vector, the lag set is \(\mathcal{L} = p\) and the user can skip intermediate lags. AR(p = c(1, 12)) evaluates only the lag 1 and lag 12 coefficients, with no \(\rho_2\) through \(\rho_{11}\) in the model. Sparse lag sets are the right shape for periodic seasonal recurrence (lag 1 momentum and lag 12 annual cycle for monthly data; lag 1 and lag 7 for weekly data) without paying for the intermediate coefficients. Default priors bound each \(\rho_l\) in \((-1, 1)\). For AR(1), the initial state is rescaled to the stationary marginal variance, so the prior at \(t = 1\) matches the stationary distribution implied by \(\rho_1\) and \(\sigma_i\):

\[\begin{align*} z_{i, t} & \sim \text{Normal}\!\Bigl( \textstyle \sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}} \rho_{i, l} \, z_{i, t - l} + \theta_i \, \epsilon_{i, t - 1},\; \sigma_i \Bigr) \end{align*}\]

Vector autoregressions: VAR(p = ...)

VAR() generalises AR() to multiple correlated series. Each \(A_l\) is an \(N_{\text{series}} \times N_{\text{series}}\) transition matrix. Entries off the diagonal carry the lagged effect of one series on another. Unlike AR(), VAR() requires p to be a scalar (VAR(p = 2) includes lags 1 and 2): the Heaps (2023) joint stationary prior used here needs consecutive companion-form structure, so sparse lag vectors are not supported. Innovations are correlated through \(\Sigma\), with an LKJ prior on the Cholesky factor:

\[\begin{align*} z_t & \sim \text{Normal}\!\Bigl( \textstyle \sum_{l=1}^{p} A_l \, z_{t - l},\; \Sigma \Bigr) \end{align*}\]

Zero-mean multivariate normal: ZMVN()

ZMVN() is VAR() stripped of its temporal recursion. It is the right kernel when the data are organised by a non-temporal grouping (sites or regions, for example) and the question is residual covariance rather than dynamics, which is the typical setup for joint species distribution models that have no time dimension:

\[\begin{align*} z_t & \sim \text{Normal}(0, \Sigma) \end{align*}\]

Continuous-time AR(1): CAR()

For data sampled at irregular intervals, where the discrete AR recursion no longer makes sense, CAR() runs an AR(1) recursion whose lag coefficient decays geometrically with the time gap \(\Delta_t = t - t_{-}\) between the current observation and the most recent one. This is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process specialised to discrete observations:

\[\begin{align*} z_{i, t} & \sim \text{Normal}\!\bigl( \rho_i^{\Delta_t} \, z_{i, t_{-}},\; \sigma_i \bigr) \end{align*}\]

Piecewise growth: PW()

Where the trends above are stochastic, PW() is deterministic. The constructor compiles the Prophet kernel of Taylor and Letham (2018). It fits a piecewise linear or piecewise logistic trajectory with growth rate changes at a set of changepoints \(\{s_1, \dots, s_S\}\). The lag-zero rate is \(k\), the offset is \(m\), and the magnitude shift at changepoint \(s_j\) is \(\delta_j\). Let \(a(t)\) be the \(\mathbb{R}^S\) indicator with \(a_j(t) = \mathbb{1}\{t \geq s_j\}\) and \(\gamma\) the offset-correction vector that keeps the trajectory continuous. Linear growth:

\[\begin{align*} z_t & = \bigl(k + a(t)^\top \delta\bigr) t + \bigl(m + a(t)^\top \gamma\bigr) \end{align*}\]

Logistic growth uses the same \((k, m, \delta)\) but maps through a sigmoid with a (possibly time-varying) carrying capacity \(C(t)\):

\[\begin{align*} z_t & = \frac{C(t)}{1 + \exp\!\bigl( -(k + a(t)^\top \delta)\,(t - (m + a(t)^\top \gamma)) \bigr)} \end{align*}\]

Composing options

Three orthogonal switches layer on top of any per-series trend, so the six constructors above cover many configurations without new kernels.

The first is cor = TRUE, which switches independent innovations to correlated innovations through an LKJ prior on the Cholesky factor of \(\Sigma\).

The second is gr = / subgr =, which sets up hierarchical residual correlation matrices. The per-group covariance is shrunk toward a population matrix through a weight \(\alpha_{\text{cor}} \in (0, 1)\) on the population draw:

\[\begin{align*} \Omega_g & = \alpha_{\text{cor}} \, \Omega_{\text{global}} + (1 - \alpha_{\text{cor}}) \, \Omega_{g, \text{local}} \end{align*}\]

The third is n_lv < n_series, which moves the trend into a factor model. A \(K\)-dimensional latent process \(\tilde{z}_t\) drives the \(N_{\text{series}}\)-dimensional state through a loadings matrix \(Z \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{series}} \times K}\):

\[\begin{align*} z_t & = Z \, \tilde{z}_t, \qquad \tilde{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^K \end{align*}\]

Factor structure delivers three advantages over running \(N_{\text{series}}\) independent latent processes. The first is dimensionality reduction: communities with dozens of correlated outcomes (species, financial assets, regions) can share dynamics through a small set of latent axes, cutting parameter count and identifying joint structure that fully-independent fits miss.

The second is rotation invariance. The marginal covariance \(Z Z^\top\) identifies what the data can resolve about \(Z\), and mvgam follows the Heaps and Jermyn (2024) prior: sample \(Z\) unconstrained, then apply a thin QR rotation in generated quantities to identify \(Z\) post hoc. The diagonal of the rotated loadings is non-negative by construction. This removes the \(2^K\) sign equivalence that constrained factor models suffer from. For VAR factor models the lag-coefficient cube rotates the same way: \(\tilde{A}_l = Q \, A_l \, Q^\top\) for the orthogonal \(Q\) delivered by the QR factorisation. Per-factor scalar parameters (\(\rho_l\), \(\sigma\), MA coefficients, \(L_{\Omega}\)) stay in the unrotated latent basis.

The third is structured priors. loadings_prior accepts either a Matrix Normal prior built from per-series features and distance matrices, or the multiplicative gamma process (MGP) prior of Bhattacharya and Dunson (2011). MGP shrinks later columns of \(Z\) toward zero, so n_lv becomes a truncation ceiling on the factor count. Under MGP, active_factors() reports the posterior distribution of the number of active columns.

See ?mvgam_trends for the full surface, ?AR, ?VAR, ?RW, ?ZMVN, ?CAR, ?PW for the per-constructor arguments, ?loadings_prior for the structured priors, and ?ordinate.mvgam for biplot helpers on the rotated loadings. Smooth Gaussian process effects on covariates remain available through gp() inside either formula or trend_formula.

The example below uses AR(p = c(1, 12)) to fit lag-1 momentum together with annual recurrence at lag 12 from monthly data, which is the dynamic structure the simulator was set up to generate.

A simulated example

sim_mvgam(type = 7) generates a single Gaussian series with a deterministic seasonal cycle on the obs side and a sparse-lag AR(p = c(1, 12)) latent state. The seasonal cycle is identifiable from the smooth alone. The latent state carries momentum at lag 1 and recurrence at lag 12 on top of that fixed cycle. The simulator returns 240 monthly observations and holds the final 12 back as a test set, so the question “does adding the latent state help on a forecast the model has never seen?” has somewhere to land.

set.seed(7)
sim <- sim_mvgam(
  type               = 7L,
  family             = gaussian(),
  n_series           = 1L,
  n_timepoints       = 240L,
  proportional_train = 12 / 13
)

The next two sections fit exactly that fixed-seasonal model and then add the latent state back in to recover what the smooth cannot see.

Model 1: seasonal smooth, no latent state

The first model fits the cyclic seasonal smooth and treats everything else as observation noise:

\[\begin{align*} y_t & \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_t, \sigma) \\ \mu_t & = \alpha + s_{\text{season}}(\text{season}_t) \\ \beta_{\text{smooth}} & \sim \text{Normal}\!\bigl(0, (S \lambda)^{-1}\bigr) \\ \sigma & \sim \text{half-}t_3(0, 2.5) \end{align*}\]

The cyclic smooth wraps January (1) onto December (12) through the bs = "cc" basis; the knots argument places the cycle endpoints just outside the observed range so the basis treats month 1 and month 12 as adjacent.

step1 <- mvgam(
  y ~ s(season, bs = "cc", k = 12),
  knots   = list(season = c(0.5, 12.5)),
  family  = gaussian(),
  data    = train,
  newdata = test,
  chains  = 2,
  samples = 400,
  burnin  = 400,
  silent  = 2
)

The headline number is sigma: roughly \(3.2\) on the response scale. The cyclic smooth has been pulled toward zero (sds_1 posterior median around \(0.09\), smooth basis coefficients all close to zero) because most of the temporal variation in the response is unstructured to a fit that ignores time. With nothing else to explain that variation, the obs noise absorbs it.

conditional_effects(step1)

The marginal smooth is essentially flat. The Dunn-Smyth residuals show why this fit is misspecified:

plot(step1, type = "residuals")

Lag-1 and lag-12 autocorrelation are both visible in the residual ACF. The dynamic structure that the simulator built into the data has nowhere to go in this fit, so it leaks into the residuals and pushes sigma up. A state-space model would absorb that signal into a latent state and leave the residuals clean.

Model 2: add an AR(1, 12) latent state

The second model keeps the seasonal smooth and lets the latent state carry the structure model 1 missed:

\[\begin{align*} y_t & \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_t + z_t, \sigma) \\ \mu_t & = \alpha + s_{\text{season}}(\text{season}_t) \\ z_t & \sim \text{Normal}\!\bigl(\rho_1 \, z_{t - 1} + \rho_{12} \, z_{t - 12}, \sigma_{\text{trend}}\bigr) \\ \rho_1, \rho_{12} & \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1)[-1, 1] \\ \sigma_{\text{trend}} & \sim \text{half-}t_3(0, 2.5) \end{align*}\]

The obs-side formula is unchanged; the latent state enters through trend_formula:

step2 <- mvgam(
  y ~ s(season, bs = "cc", k = 12),
  knots         = list(season = c(0.5, 12.5)),
  trend_formula = ~ AR(p = c(1, 12)),
  family        = gaussian(),
  data          = train,
  newdata       = test,
  chains        = 2,
  samples       = 400,
  burnin        = 400,
  silent        = 2
)

  prob_gt_0 = c(mean(ar1 > 0), mean(ar12 > 0))

#>    parameter truth    median  lower_90  upper_90 prob_gt_0
#> 1  ar1_trend  0.55 0.5330529 0.4003452 0.6658101    1.0000
#> 2 ar12_trend  0.40 0.2648392 0.1602951 0.3698621    0.9995

Both lag coefficients are clearly above zero, with posterior probability \(1.0\) for each. The 90% intervals cover the simulator truths of \(0.55\) and \(0.40\). mcmc_plot() returns the same posteriors as densities:

mcmc_plot(step2, variable = "ar[0-9]+_trend",
          regex = TRUE, type = "areas")

With the dynamic structure now carried by the latent state, the Dunn-Smyth residuals no longer show the lag-1 and lag-12 autocorrelation that model 1’s residuals carried:

plot(step2, type = "residuals")

And the cyclic smooth, freed from the job of explaining time-varying structure that wasn’t seasonal, identifies the underlying annual cycle:

conditional_effects(step2)

Hindcast versus forecast

The fit produces two posterior trajectories that get separate names. hindcast() returns the trajectory at the training times, read directly from the Stan-fitted latent state and therefore deterministic at every draw. forecast() propagates the AR(1, 12) recursion forward from the last fitted state to held-out times, accumulating uncertainty from sigma_trend and the obs noise. Both return a single mvgam_forecast object with the same structure; the forecast arm is populated only when newdata covers times beyond the training window:

fc <- forecast(step2, newdata = test)
dim(fc$hindcasts[[1]])
#> [1] 2000  221
dim(fc$forecasts[[1]])
#> [1] 2000   19
plot(fc, series = 1)

The forecast trajectory is the held-out test the rest of the analysis hinges on, and the next two sections grade it.

Forecast scoring across rules

Proper scoring rules grade probabilistic forecasts on calibration and sharpness in one number. score() returns one score per timepoint from an mvgam_forecast object. Summing across the held-out window gives a single number per model that can be compared across fits. The Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) is the default, but the same call supports the log score (logs), the Dawid-Sebastiani score (dss), and the Discrete Ranked Probability Score (drps):

fc1 <- forecast(step1, newdata = test)
fc2 <- forecast(step2, newdata = test)
score_rules <- c("crps", "logs", "dss", "drps")
scores <- vapply(score_rules, function(rule) {
  s1 <- score(fc1, score = rule)
  s2 <- score(fc2, score = rule)
  c(no_trend = sum(s1[[1]]$score, na.rm = TRUE),
    ar_1_12  = sum(s2[[1]]$score, na.rm = TRUE))
}, numeric(2))
round(scores, 2)
#>           crps  logs   dss  drps
#> no_trend 41.32 52.03 69.78 13.83
#> ar_1_12  38.02 50.21 66.26 11.84

A lower score is better. The state-space fit wins on every rule. CRPS drops by roughly \(3.4\), the log score by \(1.3\), the Dawid-Sebastiani score by \(4.0\), and the DRPS by \(1.9\). DSS shows the biggest margin because that rule penalises poorly calibrated forecast variance most aggressively, and the trendless fit stays overconfident around its (flat) cycle.

score() covers additional rules through the score argument: the interval score (sis) and the quantile score (qs) for calibration tasks; twcrps weights CRPS toward tail behaviour. For multivariate forecasts from VAR fits or factor models, score() also supports energy (energy score) and variogram (variogram score); both penalise dependence patterns that per-series rules miss. The interval_width argument sets the nominal coverage band used by the interval-based rules.

Leave-future-out cross-validation

A single train/test split rewards models that happen to win on one specific horizon. lfo_cv.mvgam() automates the alternative: it scores forecasts at every refit point along the series, then aggregates the per-step scores across many forecast origins.

lfo_step2 <- lfo_cv(step2, min_t = 200, fc_horizon = 12,
                    silent = 2)
plot(lfo_step2)

min_t sets the first refit point, fc_horizon sets the prediction window per fold, and the chain is reseated through update() between folds so the Stan program does not recompile. The default score is the expected log predictive density (ELPD), returned per fold and per step ahead. The companion kfold.mvgam() runs block CV for grouped designs through cluster-level holdout. It supports user-defined fold maps through the folds argument.

loo() and loo_compare() also dispatch on mvgam objects, but both inherit the same pathology when applied to state-space fits. PSIS-LOO approximates leaving \(y_t\) out of the likelihood, but the chain still conditioned on \(y_t\) when it estimated the latent state \(z_t\). The approximate density is therefore evaluated against a posterior that has already seen the observation through the latent path; Pareto-\(k\) diagnostics warn about exactly this, and differencing two contaminated ELPDs in loo_compare() does not remove the contamination. The honest comparison uses either the forecast score above on the held-out window or lfo_cv() below. lfo_cv() refits the chain on shorter prefixes, so the latent state at the scoring time was never conditioned on its own observation.
