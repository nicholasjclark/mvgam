                                        Abstract
     Supplementary material contained in this note include proofs and derivations
     of the results used in the paper, complete descriptions of the adaptive Gibbs
     samplers, further background and results on the simulation experiment and two
     applications.

     Keywords: Covariance matrix, Dimension reduction, Intraday gas demand, Latent
     factor models, Stationary dynamic factor models, Structured prior distributions

     MSC Classification: 62F15 , 62J99 , 62M10




S1 Prior for the identified factor loadings matrix
Section 3 of the main manuscript describes two prior distributions for the uncon-
strained and unidentified factor loadings matrix Λ ∈ Rp×k : (i) a matrix-normal prior,
Λ ∼ Np,k (0, Φ, Ψ) and (ii) a matrix-t prior, Λ ∼ tp,k (ς, 0, Φ̆, Ψ). An identifiable factor
loadings matrix Λ̃ ∈ Rp×k , which satisfies the PLT constraint, can be calculated from
Λ by taking a LQ decomposition, Λ = Λ̃Q, in which Q is a k × k orthogonal matrix.
This decomposition will be unique as long as p ≥ k and Λ is full rank. Therefore, we
can, in principle, calculate the prior density induced for the identifiable factor loadings
matrix Λ̃ by computing the joint density for (Λ̃, Q) and then integrating out Q.

As in the static case, the adaptive Gibbs sampler for a dynamic model begins with
a sweep through all the steps of the non-adaptive scheme. In the adaptation step, an
extra complication arises when the truncation point H is increased by 1. Ideally, we
would add a row and column to the parameters of the state equation, Γ1 , . . . , Γm ,
by sampling from their prior, conditional on the submatrices that already occupy the
first H rows and columns. However, in order to enforce stationarity, we work with a
reparameterisation of the vector autoregressive model for the factors. Priors are placed
on the unconstrained parameters A1 , . . . , Am on which the parameters in the original
model, Γ1 , . . . , Γm , Π, depend. Due to the recursive nature of the mapping between
the original parameters and the intermediate partial autocorrelation parameterisation
P 1 , . . . , P m , the Jacobian of the transformation is not available in closed form and so
neither is the prior for the original parameters over the stationary region. We cannot,
therefore, simply sample extra rows and columns of the Γi from their prior.
     As an alternative, we propose basing the adaptation on the parsimonious assump-
tion that the new factor does not interact with the existing factors at lags 1, . . . , m
and interacts minimally with itself. This requires filling the first H elements in row
and column H + 1 of the adapted matrices Γ1 , . . . , Γm with zeros. Equivalently, we
can fill the first H elements in row and column H + 1 of the adapted partial autocorre-
lation matrices P 1 , . . . , P m with zeros. The partial autocorrelation matrices lie in the
space of square matrices whose singular values are all less than one. We can therefore
guarantee a new parameter set that lies in the stationary region whilst adhering to
our adaptation assumptions by simulating the (H + 1, H + 1) element in the adapted
P i by sampling uniformly on (0, ri,min ) where ri,min is the smallest singular value of
P i,1:H,1:H . We can then compute the corresponding value for Π using step 2 of the
reverse mapping in the Appendix of Heaps [8].

S3.3 Post-processing the posterior output
Although the MCMC samplers described in this work generate draws from the pos-
terior of the unknowns in the unidentified and unconstrained parameterisation of
the factor model, an identified parameterisation is incredibly valuable for parame-
ter interpretation and the assessment of MCMC diagnostics. Fortunately, samples of
the unknowns in the unidentified and unconstrained parameterisation can be post-
processed to obtain samples under a parameterisation in which the factor loadings
matrix is identified by the PLT constraint. Denote by η̃ i the factors in an identified
model with corresponding factor loadings matrix Λ̃ which satisfies the PLT condition.
In the dynamic model, additionally denote by Γ̃1 , . . . , Γ̃m and Π̃ the autoregres-
sive coefficients and innovation variance in the corresponding state equation, and by
Ã1 , . . . , Ãm the transformed partial autocorrelation matrices. Now, for each sample
from the posterior, a set of identifiable parameters can be found by calculating the
LQ decomposition of Λ, Λ = Λ̃Q, and then transforming η̃ i = Qη i for i = 1, . . . , n
and, in the dynamic case, Γ̃i = QΓi QT , i = 1, . . . , m, and Π̃ = QΠQT . Equivalently,
in terms of the unconstrained reparameterisation of the stationary state equation,
Ãi = QAi QT for i = 1, . . . , m.

S4.2 Co-occurrence of Finnish birds
S4.2.1 Further comments on the model and prior
As remarked in the main manuscript, the meta-covariates and branch lengths are
standardised so that all length-scale parameters ϑ1 , . . . , ϑ5 operate on a comparable
scale. To this end, before calculating the distances dC,ij in the space of meta-covariates,
we standardise the single continuous variable, namely the logarithm of body mass
(x1,4 , . . . , x50,4 ), by dividing the measurements by their standard deviation. In terms
of the evolutionary information, the phylogenetic tree in this example is ultrametric
and so the root to tip distance is the same for all leaves. As such, before calculating
dP,ij , the branch lengths are standardised by scaling to make the common root to tip
distance one.
    In order to facilitate comparison with inferences under the structured increasing
shrinkage (SIS) process prior, our prior for the regression coefficients B determining
the area-specific means µi = B T wi is identical to that chosen in Schiavon et al.
[11]. Specifically, structured borrowing of information between species is allowed by
taking B T |K ∼ Np,c (XK, I p , s2β I c ) in which s2β = 1 and K = (κij ) is a q × c
matrix of coefficients. These coefficients are themselves assigned a prior such that
κij ∼ N(0, s2κ ) independently in which s2κ = 1. Finally, following Schiavon et al. the
continuous covariates in W and X are standardised.

S4.3.1 Model for the time-varying mean
The main factors that are known to influence the mean level of residential demand for
gas are the weather along with seasonal and calendar effects [15]. Each term in the

time-varying mean µt = (µt1 , . . . , µt,24 )T can be written as

                       µti = β1i + (β2i + β3i w̃t )wt2 + β4i wt4 + χti + ϖti

where w̃t is the temperature for day t and wt2 = w̃t − md(t) in which md(t) is a
smoothed annual average for the temperature on day t where d(t) ∈ {1, 2, . . . , 366}.
We include both the mean-centered temperature, wt2 , and its interaction with the raw
temperature, wt3 = w̃t wt2 , to allow the effect of above or below average temperatures
to differ according to absolute weather conditions. The variable wt4 is an indicator
that is equal to 1 if day t is a public holiday and equal to zero otherwise.
